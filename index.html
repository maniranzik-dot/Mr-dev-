Unit 6: Mitosis and Meiosis

6.1 Cell Division

Cell division is the process by which a parent cell divides to produce new cells. The two important types of cell division are mitosis and meiosis.

6.2 Mitosis

Mitosis is a type of cell division that produces two genetically identical daughter cells with the same chromosome number as the parent cell.

6.3 Stages of Mitosis

  • Prophase: Chromosomes become visible and the nuclear membrane breaks down.
  • Metaphase: Chromosomes arrange themselves at the equator of the cell.
  • Anaphase: Sister chromatids separate and move to opposite poles.
  • Telophase: New nuclei form around the chromosomes.
  • Cytokinesis: The cytoplasm divides to form two daughter cells.

6.4 Importance of Mitosis

  • Growth of organisms
  • Replacement of damaged cells
  • Repair of tissues
  • Asexual reproduction in some organisms

6.5 Meiosis

Meiosis is a type of cell division that produces four genetically different haploid cells from one diploid cell.

6.6 Importance of Meiosis

  • Formation of gametes
  • Maintains chromosome number between generations
  • Creates genetic variation
Key difference: Mitosis produces 2 genetically similar cells, while meiosis produces 4 genetically different haploid cells.
Exercise:

Give two differences between mitosis and meiosis.

Mitosis produces two genetically similar cells, while meiosis produces four genetically different cells. Mitosis maintains the chromosome number, while meiosis reduces it by half.

Unit 7: Heterotrophic Nutrition

7.1 Meaning of Heterotrophic Nutrition

Heterotrophic nutrition is a mode of nutrition in which organisms obtain organic food from other organisms because they cannot manufacture their own food.

7.2 Types of Heterotrophic Nutrition

  • Holozoic nutrition
  • Saprophytic nutrition
  • Parasitic nutrition
  • Symbiotic nutrition

7.3 Holozoic Nutrition

Holozoic nutrition involves taking in solid or liquid food and digesting it internally.

Ingestion → Digestion → Absorption → Assimilation → Egestion

7.4 Human Digestive System

  • Mouth
  • Oesophagus
  • Stomach
  • Small intestine
  • Large intestine
  • Rectum
  • Anus

7.5 Functions of the Digestive Organs

Mouth: Food is chewed and mixed with saliva.
Stomach: Food is mixed with gastric juice and partially digested.
Small intestine: Digestion is completed and nutrients are absorbed.
Large intestine: Water is absorbed and faeces are formed.

7.6 Digestive Enzymes

  • Amylase breaks down starch.
  • Protease breaks down proteins.
  • Lipase breaks down fats.
Exercise:

What is heterotrophic nutrition?

Heterotrophic nutrition is a mode of nutrition in which organisms obtain food from other organisms.

Unit 8: Circulatory Systems in Humans

8.1 Meaning of Circulation

Circulation is the movement of substances such as oxygen, nutrients, hormones and waste products around the body.

8.2 Components of the Circulatory System

  • Heart
  • Blood
  • Blood vessels

8.3 The Human Heart

The heart is a muscular organ that pumps blood around the body.

8.4 Chambers of the Heart

  • Right atrium
  • Right ventricle
  • Left atrium
  • Left ventricle

8.5 Blood Vessels

Arteries: Carry blood away from the heart.
Veins: Carry blood towards the heart.
Capillaries: Very small blood vessels where exchange of substances occurs.

8.6 Components of Blood

  • Red blood cells
  • White blood cells
  • Platelets
  • Plasma

8.7 Functions of Blood

  • Transport of oxygen
  • Transport of nutrients
  • Removal of waste products
  • Protection against diseases
  • Blood clotting
  • Distribution of heat
Exercise:

What is the main function of red blood cells?

Red blood cells transport oxygen from the lungs to body tissues using haemoglobin.

Unit 9: Cellular Respiration

9.1 Meaning of Cellular Respiration

Cellular respiration is the process by which cells release energy from food, especially glucose, to produce ATP.

9.2 Aerobic Respiration

Aerobic respiration occurs in the presence of oxygen and releases a large amount of energy.

Glucose + Oxygen → Carbon dioxide + Water + Energy

9.3 Anaerobic Respiration

Anaerobic respiration occurs without oxygen and releases less energy than aerobic respiration.

9.4 Anaerobic Respiration in Yeast

Glucose → Ethanol + Carbon dioxide + Energy

9.5 Anaerobic Respiration in Muscles

Glucose → Lactic acid + Energy

9.6 Importance of Respiration

  • Provides energy for movement.
  • Provides energy for active transport.
  • Provides energy for growth.
  • Provides energy for reproduction.
  • Provides energy for synthesis of substances.
Exercise:

What is the difference between aerobic and anaerobic respiration?

Aerobic respiration requires oxygen and releases more energy, while anaerobic respiration occurs without oxygen and releases less energy.

Unit 10: Skin and Homeostatic Mechanisms

10.1 The Skin

The skin is the largest organ of the human body. It protects the body and helps regulate body temperature.

10.2 Functions of the Skin

  • Protection
  • Temperature regulation
  • Sensation
  • Excretion of sweat
  • Prevention of excessive water loss

10.3 Temperature Regulation

The human body maintains its internal temperature within a narrow range. This is an example of homeostasis.

10.4 When Body Temperature Increases

  • Sweat glands produce more sweat.
  • Blood vessels near the skin widen (vasodilation).
  • More heat is lost from the skin.

10.5 When Body Temperature Decreases

  • Sweating decreases.
  • Blood vessels near the skin narrow (vasoconstriction).
  • Muscles contract rapidly, causing shivering.
  • Metabolic activity can increase to produce heat.

10.6 Homeostasis

Homeostasis is the maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment despite changes in the external environment.

Examples of homeostasis: Regulation of body temperature, blood glucose concentration and water balance.

10.7 Negative Feedback

Negative feedback is a control mechanism in which a change in a condition causes responses that return the condition towards its normal level.
