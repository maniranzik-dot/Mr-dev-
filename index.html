Unit 6: Chemical Properties of Acids and Bases

6.1 Preparation of Acids and Bases

Acids are substances that produce hydrogen ions (H⁺) when dissolved in water. Bases produce hydroxide ions (OH⁻) in aqueous solution.

6.2 Reaction of Acids with Water and Sodium Metal

Acids react with water to form hydronium ions. Reactive metals such as sodium react with water to form an alkaline solution and hydrogen gas.

Example:

2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂

6.3 Reactions of Acids and Bases

Acids react with bases in a neutralisation reaction to form salt and water.

Acid + Base → Salt + Water

6.4 Uses and Dangers of Acids and Bases

  • Acids are used in batteries and industries.
  • Bases are used in soap and detergent production.
  • Strong acids and bases can cause burns.
  • They should be handled carefully in the laboratory.
Exercise:

What is neutralisation?

Neutralisation is the reaction between an acid and a base to form salt and water.

Unit 7: Concentration of Solutions

7.1 Concept of Concentration

Concentration describes the amount of solute dissolved in a given volume of solution.

Concentration = Amount of solute / Volume of solution

7.2 Preparation of Solutions

A solution can be prepared by dissolving a known amount of solute in a known volume of solvent.

7.3 Acids and Titrations

Titration is a laboratory method used to determine the concentration of an unknown solution by reacting it with a solution of known concentration.

M₁V₁ = M₂V₂
Exercise:

What is a solution?

A solution is a homogeneous mixture of a solute and a solvent.

Unit 8: Electrolysis and Its Applications

8.1 Introduction to Oxidation-Reduction Reactions

Oxidation is the loss of electrons, while reduction is the gain of electrons.

OIL = Oxidation Is Loss
RIG = Reduction Is Gain

8.2 Electrolysis of Solutions

Electrolysis is the chemical decomposition of an ionic compound using electricity.

8.3 Electrolysis of Molten Compounds

Molten ionic compounds contain freely moving ions and can conduct electricity. Positive ions move toward the cathode and negative ions move toward the anode.

8.4 Applications of Electrolysis

  • Electroplating
  • Extraction of metals
  • Purification of metals
  • Manufacture of chemicals
Exercise:

What is electrolysis?

Electrolysis is the decomposition of an ionic compound using electrical energy.

Unit 9: Structure and Properties of Alkenes and Alcohols

9.1 Nomenclature of Alkenes and Their Structures

Alkenes are unsaturated hydrocarbons containing at least one carbon-carbon double bond.

General formula of alkenes: CₙH₂ₙ

9.2 Sources of Alkenes

Alkenes can be obtained from the cracking of large hydrocarbon molecules.

9.3 Properties of Alkenes

  • They contain a carbon-carbon double bond.
  • They are generally more reactive than alkanes.
  • They undergo addition reactions.
  • They can be used to manufacture polymers.

9.4 Uses of Alkenes

  • Manufacture of plastics
  • Production of alcohols
  • Production of other chemicals

9.5 Structure of Alcohols

Alcohols are organic compounds containing a hydroxyl group (–OH).

9.6 Preparation of Alcohols

Alcohols can be prepared by fermentation of sugars or by hydration of alkenes.

9.7 Properties of Alcohols

  • Many alcohols are soluble in water.
  • They are useful solvents.
  • They can burn to produce heat.

9.8 Uses and Misuse of Alcohol

  • Used as solvents.
  • Used as fuels.
  • Used in some medicines and disinfectants.
  • Excessive consumption can cause health and social problems.
Exercise:

What is an alkene?

An alkene is an unsaturated hydrocarbon containing a carbon-carbon double bond.
