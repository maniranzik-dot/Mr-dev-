Unit 1: Carbon and its Inorganic Compounds

1.1 Carbon and Allotropy

Carbon is a chemical element with symbol C and atomic number 6. It has four valence electrons and can form many compounds.

Allotropy is the existence of an element in two or more different structural forms in the same physical state.

  • Diamond – very hard and does not conduct electricity.
  • Graphite – soft and conducts electricity.
  • Fullerene – carbon atoms arranged in cage-like structures.

1.2 Chemical Properties of Carbon

  • Carbon burns in oxygen to form carbon dioxide: C + O₂ → CO₂
  • With limited oxygen, carbon monoxide can form: 2C + O₂ → 2CO
  • Carbon can act as a reducing agent in some metal oxide reactions.

1.3 Inorganic Compounds of Carbon

  • Carbon dioxide (CO₂)
  • Carbon monoxide (CO)
  • Carbonates such as CaCO₃
  • Hydrogen carbonates such as NaHCO₃

1.4 Preparation and Testing of Carbonates

Carbonates react with dilute acids to produce salt, water and carbon dioxide.

Carbonate + acid → salt + water + carbon dioxide

Carbon dioxide is tested by passing it through limewater. The limewater turns milky.

1.5–1.8 Environmental Issues and Carbon Cycle

  • Carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas.
  • Excess CO₂ contributes to global warming and climate change.
  • The carbon cycle moves carbon between the atmosphere, living organisms, soil, oceans and rocks.
Exercise: What is an allotrope?

An allotrope is one of two or more different structural forms of the same element in the same physical state.

Unit 2: Nitrogen and its Inorganic Compounds

2.1 Industrial Isolation of Nitrogen

Nitrogen is obtained industrially mainly from air. Air is cooled and liquefied, then its components are separated by fractional distillation of liquid air.

2.2 Laboratory Preparation of Nitrogen

Nitrogen can be prepared in the laboratory using reactions that remove oxygen from air or produce nitrogen from suitable compounds.

2.3 Properties and Uses of Nitrogen

  • Nitrogen is a colourless, odourless gas.
  • It is relatively unreactive at room temperature.
  • It is used in food packaging and production of ammonia.
  • It is used in some industrial processes.

2.4 Environmental Issues

Nitrogen compounds can contribute to air pollution, acid rain and eutrophication when released excessively into the environment.

Exercise: Why is nitrogen separated from air industrially?

Nitrogen is needed for many industrial applications, especially ammonia manufacture and food preservation.

Unit 3: Sulphur and its Inorganic Compounds

3.1 Occurrence, Extraction, Properties and Uses of Sulphur

Sulphur occurs naturally in minerals and underground deposits. It is a yellow non-metal and has several important industrial uses.

  • Manufacture of sulphuric acid
  • Fungicides and pesticides
  • Production of some chemicals

3.2 Inorganic Compounds of Sulphur

  • Hydrogen sulphide (H₂S)
  • Sulphur dioxide (SO₂)
  • Sulphur trioxide (SO₃)
  • Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄)

3.3 Industrial Preparation of Sulphuric Acid

The Contact Process is used industrially. Sulphur is converted to sulphur dioxide, then to sulphur trioxide, which is used to produce sulphuric acid.

S + O₂ → SO₂
2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃

3.4 Tests for Sulphates and Sulphites

Sulphate ions can be tested using a suitable barium salt solution in acidic conditions. A white precipitate of barium sulphate indicates sulphate.

3.5 Environmental Issues

Sulphur dioxide released from burning sulphur-containing fuels can contribute to air pollution and acid rain.

Exercise: Name the industrial process used to manufacture sulphuric acid.

The Contact Process.

Unit 4: Chlorine and its Inorganic Compounds

4.1 Preparation and Test for Chlorine

Chlorine is a greenish-yellow gas with a characteristic smell. In the laboratory it can be prepared by oxidising chloride ions using a suitable oxidising agent.

4.2 Chemical Properties of Chlorine

  • Chlorine reacts with metals to form chlorides.
  • It reacts with hydrogen to form hydrogen chloride.
  • It reacts with water and can have bleaching and disinfecting effects.

4.3 Uses of Chlorine

  • Water disinfection
  • Manufacture of chemicals
  • Bleaching
  • Production of some plastics and solvents

4.4 Compounds and Halides of Chlorine

Chlorine forms compounds such as hydrogen chloride (HCl), sodium chloride (NaCl), hypochlorites and other chlorides.

4.5 Environmental Issues

Chlorine-containing chemicals must be handled carefully because some can be harmful to living organisms and the environment.

Exercise: Give one use of chlorine.

One use is disinfecting drinking water.

Unit 5: Rate of Reactions

5.1 Definition of Reaction Rate

The rate of a chemical reaction describes how quickly reactants are converted into products.

Rate = change in amount or concentration ÷ time

Factors Affecting the Rate of Reaction

  • Temperature: Increasing temperature usually increases reaction rate.
  • Concentration: Increasing concentration can increase collision frequency.
  • Surface area: Smaller particles generally react faster because more surface is exposed.
  • Pressure: For gases, increasing pressure can increase collision frequency.
  • Catalyst: A catalyst increases reaction rate by providing an alternative pathway with lower activation energy.

Collision Theory

Particles must collide with sufficient energy and suitable orientation for an effective reaction to occur.

Increasing the number of successful collisions generally increases the reaction rate.

Exercise: State two factors that affect the rate of a chemical reaction.

Examples include temperature, concentration, surface area, pressure for gases, and the presence of a catalyst.
