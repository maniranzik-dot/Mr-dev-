Unit 11: Response and Coordination in Plants

11.1 Response in Plants

Plants respond to changes in their environment such as light, gravity, water and touch. Plant responses help plants grow and survive.

11.2 Tropisms

A tropism is a directional growth response of a plant to an external stimulus.

  • Phototropism: Response to light.
  • Geotropism: Response to gravity.
  • Hydrotropism: Response to water.
  • Thigmotropism: Response to touch.

11.3 Phototropism

Shoots usually grow towards light and therefore show positive phototropism. Roots generally grow away from light.

11.4 Coordination in Plants

Plant hormones coordinate growth and responses in plants. Important plant hormones include auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins, abscisic acid and ethene.

Unit 12: Response and Coordination in Animals

12.1 Nervous Coordination

Animals coordinate their activities using the nervous system and hormonal system.

12.2 The Nervous System

  • Brain
  • Spinal cord
  • Peripheral nerves

12.3 Neurons

A neuron is a specialised cell that transmits electrical nerve impulses.

12.4 Reflex Actions

A reflex action is a rapid, automatic response to a stimulus.

Stimulus → Receptor → Sensory neuron → Relay neuron → Motor neuron → Effector → Response

12.5 Sense Organs

  • Eye — detects light
  • Ear — detects sound and helps with balance
  • Nose — detects chemicals in the air
  • Tongue — detects chemicals in food
  • Skin — detects touch, temperature and pain

Unit 13: Asexual and Sexual Reproduction

13.1 Reproduction

Reproduction is the biological process by which organisms produce new individuals of their own species.

13.2 Asexual Reproduction

Asexual reproduction involves one parent and does not involve the fusion of gametes.

  • Binary fission
  • Budding
  • Vegetative propagation
  • Spore formation

13.3 Advantages of Asexual Reproduction

  • Only one parent is required.
  • It is usually fast.
  • Many offspring can be produced quickly.
  • Useful characteristics can be preserved.

13.4 Sexual Reproduction

Sexual reproduction involves the fusion of male and female gametes. It produces genetic variation among offspring.

13.5 Differences

Asexual → One parent → No gamete fusion → Little genetic variation
Sexual → Usually two parents → Gamete fusion → Genetic variation
Exercise:

Give one difference between sexual and asexual reproduction.

Asexual reproduction usually involves one parent and no fusion of gametes, while sexual reproduction involves fusion of male and female gametes.

Unit 14: Sexual Reproduction in Flowering Plants

14.1 Structure of a Flower

  • Sepals
  • Petals
  • Stamen
  • Carpel

14.2 Male Reproductive Part

The stamen is the male reproductive structure of a flower. It consists of the anther and filament.

14.3 Female Reproductive Part

The carpel is the female reproductive structure. It consists of the stigma, style and ovary.

14.4 Pollination

Pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from the anther to the stigma.

  • Self-pollination
  • Cross-pollination

14.5 Fertilisation

Fertilisation occurs when the male gamete fuses with the female gamete inside the ovule to form a zygote.

14.6 Seed and Fruit Formation

After fertilisation, the ovule develops into a seed while the ovary usually develops into a fruit.

Exercise:

What is pollination?

Pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from the anther to the stigma of a flower.

Unit 15: Reproduction in Human Beings

15.1 Male Reproductive System

  • Testes
  • Scrotum
  • Sperm ducts
  • Urethra
  • Penis
  • Accessory glands

15.2 Female Reproductive System

  • Ovaries
  • Oviducts
  • Uterus
  • Cervix
  • Vagina

15.3 Gametes

The male gamete is the sperm. The female gamete is the ovum. Gametes contain half the normal number of chromosomes.

15.4 Menstrual Cycle

The menstrual cycle prepares the female reproductive system for possible pregnancy. It involves changes in the ovaries and uterus controlled by hormones.

15.5 Fertilisation

Fertilisation is the fusion of a sperm and an ovum to form a zygote.

Sperm + Ovum → Zygote

15.6 Pregnancy

After fertilisation, the developing embryo becomes implanted in the lining of the uterus. The placenta allows exchange of substances between the mother and developing fetus.

15.7 Birth

Birth involves contractions of the uterus, opening of the cervix and delivery of the baby.

15.8 Reproductive Health

  • Maintaining personal hygiene
  • Seeking reliable health information
  • Preventing sexually transmitted infections
  • Making responsible reproductive decisions
  • Seeking appropriate healthcare
Exercise:

Where does fertilisation normally occur in humans?

Fertilisation normally occurs in the oviduct (fallopian tube).
