Unit 4: Renewable and Non-Renewable Energy Resources

4.1 Energy Resources

Energy resources are sources from which useful energy can be obtained. They can be classified into renewable and non-renewable resources.

4.2 Renewable Energy Resources

Renewable energy resources are sources of energy that are naturally replenished and can be used repeatedly.

  • Solar energy
  • Wind energy
  • Hydroelectric energy
  • Biomass energy
  • Geothermal energy
  • Tidal and wave energy

4.3 Solar Energy

Solar energy is energy obtained from the Sun. Solar panels can convert solar energy into electrical energy.

4.4 Wind Energy

Wind turbines convert the kinetic energy of moving air into electrical energy.

4.5 Hydroelectric Energy

Hydroelectric power uses moving or falling water to turn turbines connected to electrical generators.

4.6 Biomass Energy

Biomass energy is obtained from organic materials such as wood, agricultural waste and other plant or animal materials.

4.7 Non-Renewable Energy Resources

Non-renewable energy resources are resources that exist in limited amounts and take very long periods to form.

  • Coal
  • Petroleum
  • Natural gas
  • Nuclear fuels

4.8 Advantages of Renewable Energy

  • They are naturally replenished.
  • They can reduce dependence on fossil fuels.
  • Many produce little or no direct greenhouse-gas emissions during operation.
  • They can provide energy for remote areas.

4.9 Disadvantages of Renewable Energy

  • Some depend on weather conditions.
  • Some require high initial investment.
  • Some require large areas of land.
  • Energy storage may be needed for variable sources.

4.10 Advantages of Non-Renewable Energy

  • Many have high energy density.
  • They can provide continuous energy.
  • Existing technologies can use them efficiently.

4.11 Disadvantages of Non-Renewable Energy

  • They are finite resources.
  • Burning fossil fuels produces air pollution and greenhouse gases.
  • Extraction can damage the environment.
  • Nuclear energy produces radioactive waste.

Unit 5: Heat Transfer

5.1 Meaning of Heat

Heat is energy transferred from a body at a higher temperature to a body at a lower temperature because of the temperature difference.

5.2 Modes of Heat Transfer

Heat can be transferred in three main ways:

  • Conduction
  • Convection
  • Radiation

5.3 Conduction

Conduction is the transfer of thermal energy through a substance without overall movement of the substance from one place to another.

Metals are generally good conductors of heat, while materials such as wood, plastic and air are poor conductors.

5.4 Convection

Convection is the transfer of heat through the bulk movement of a fluid, such as a liquid or gas.

Warm fluid becomes less dense and rises, while cooler fluid becomes denser and sinks. This creates convection currents.

5.5 Radiation

Radiation is the transfer of thermal energy by electromagnetic waves. It does not require a material medium and can occur through a vacuum.

5.6 Good and Poor Absorbers

  • Dark, dull surfaces are generally good absorbers of thermal radiation.
  • Light, shiny surfaces are generally poor absorbers.

5.7 Good and Poor Emitters

  • Dark, dull surfaces are generally good emitters.
  • Light, shiny surfaces are generally poor emitters.

5.8 Applications of Heat Transfer

  • Cooking utensils
  • Thermos flasks
  • Solar heaters
  • Room ventilation
  • Refrigeration
  • Building insulation
Remember:
Conduction → mainly important in solids.
Convection → occurs in fluids.
Radiation → does not require a medium.

Unit 6: Heat Transfer and Quantities of Heat

6.1 Quantity of Heat

The quantity of heat transferred to or from a substance depends on its mass, its specific heat capacity and the change in temperature.

6.2 Specific Heat Capacity

Specific heat capacity is the amount of heat energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1°C (or 1 K).

Q = mcΔT

Where:

  • Q = quantity of heat in joules (J)
  • m = mass in kilograms (kg)
  • c = specific heat capacity in J/kg°C
  • ΔT = change in temperature in °C or K

6.3 Change in Temperature

ΔT = Final Temperature − Initial Temperature

6.4 Factors Affecting Quantity of Heat

The quantity of heat required depends on:

  • Mass of the substance
  • Specific heat capacity of the substance
  • Change in temperature

6.5 Heating and Cooling

When a substance absorbs heat, its temperature may increase. When it loses heat, its temperature may decrease, provided no phase change is occurring.

6.6 Heat Capacity

Heat capacity is the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of an entire object by 1°C (or 1 K).

C = Q ÷ ΔT

It is also related to specific heat capacity by:

C = mc

6.7 Practical Applications

  • Design of cooking utensils
  • Choice of materials for heat storage
  • Engine cooling systems
  • Heating systems
  • Thermal insulation

Exercise 1

Calculate the heat required to raise the temperature of 2 kg of water by 10°C. Take the specific heat capacity of water as 4200 J/kg°C.

Q = mcΔT

Q = 2 × 4200 × 10

Q = 84,000 J
